Phase 1: Early Ideas and Breadboarding

This project was originally intended to be a bite detector for fishing, but as time went on the main focus changed to be a mechanical stethoscope. I began by purchasing piezoelectric sesnors and breadboarding with them. As seen in Image 1, I used an Arduino Uno I already had and hooked it up to the piezoelectric on a breadboard with a 1 Megaohm resistor. I was playing around with the sensor and learning how to do some basic filtering. As I continued to progress, I added a basic second subsystem- the alert system. I started by breadboarding a buzzer, and this is where I encountered my first problem. As shown in video 2, the buzzer was not beeping when the piezoelectric went over a certain threshold. After some debugging, I changed the tone and the beep threshold and it successfully played a sound. However, that same buzzer broke and I had to purchase a new one later. After my escapade with the buzzer, I moved onto my next subsystem- the threshold system with the potentiometer. It was working to filter the noise as shown in video 3, but I wanted to scale the analog values down to 1-100 to make the shift with the potentiometer more sensitive. However, the main issue was not the code. It turns out I had a faulty potentiometer, as mentioned in video 4. Video 4 has a deeper explanation of my thought process with the potentiometer algorithm as well as my first AI iteration. In picture 5 I implenetd a capacitor to smooth the piezoelectric readings, and I also added the red LED for detection along with the transistor which I did end up getting rid of later. I did not document it with a picture, but I breadboarded with an LCD as well along with the SPST switches. I finally bridged all of my subsystems together on Tinkercad and generated a schematic to help me with the wiring later.

Phase 2: Starting the Perfboarding Process
In video 6, I started laying out all my components on a stripboard. At the time, I did not know the difference between a stripboard and perfboard, but I realized it when I had already bought the stripboard- long columns were connected. Luckily I only tested out an LED and I bought a perfboard once I realized the mistake I made. In video 7, I did the layout again except on a perfboard. Video 8 holds a timelapse of me soldering the LCD on my perfboard, and little did I know that the obstacles were aboout to arrive. Video 9 talks about the fact that I fried my first nano because I bridged GND and 5V twice due to my poor iron and skills at the time. This urged me to buy a multimeter, a tool that would help me more than I thought. Video 10 is quite long. I burned yet another nano because I did not check with my multimeter, and this was the last straw for me quite literally because I had one nano left. I check continuity between every nano pin and lcd pin in this video, and luckily none are bridged. At this point, frustration was setting in but my worst struggles were yet to come. Luckily, in Video 11 I got a break because my LCD and Nano were working without any issue. Video 12 is a big jump. I implemented my buzzer and LED along with the transistor and ON LED. It succesfully worked and the debugging was explained in the video. Video 13 was my last log for a while. In it I added the switch and the piezoelectric to the board. The video talks about my issue that I experienced with noise and high impedance- the analog wire was acting as an antenna and setting off the other systems. This was my biggest problem yet. It took me hours of multimeter testing, desoldering and soldering, and many AI iterations of code. I finally decided to make a noise baseline to bypass the main issue and I ditched the switches and transistor to simplify things. Things were working for a little bit, until I added my battery pack. The power regulation was different even though the nano was supposed to stepdown the voltage to 5V. I did a lot of signal processing to try and fix this, and here I made my hard pivot from bite detector to mechanical stethoscope. I did not want to dedicate a large amount of time placing and securing the detector to the rod and tune it to detect a bite. This would require a lot of signal processing and my messy perfboard layout already caused heavy issues with noise and bridges. Luckily, this switch was worth it as things got easier.

Phase 3: Enclosure and Final Stages
I bought a digital caliper to measure each of the gaps and used Fusion to model the final enclosure for my project. Without a doubt, there were issues with some fittings so I had to use electrical tape to secure the enclosure. Although the project is fragile, picture 14 shows a top-down view of it and video 15 shows a demonstration. I am yet to test it for its application as a mechanical stethoscope in multiple envrionments, but I am going to leave it here for now. In the future if I continue on this project I will definitely design a pcb to fix the majority of my issues. I will also clean up the enclosure and refine the code. The whole thing took me a span of 5 months because I was learning a lot of new things as I progressed- from wiring an LED to soldering 16 pins on an LCD. This project was not a full success, but it was satisfactory and I will use it as a learning experience to continue building more sophisticated projects.
